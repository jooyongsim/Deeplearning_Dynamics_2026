Particle : all mass concentrated at one point — size and rotation are ignored.

Rigid body : finite size, no deformation — rotation must always be included.

This lecture : apply kinematics and the three kinetics methods first to particles, then to rigid bodies.

The same four tools apply to both models — rigid bodies add rotation to each

The key difference : a particle never rotates; a rigid body always carries rotation — new quantities \(\omega,\ \alpha,\ I\) and one extra equation, \(\sum M_G = I_G\,\alpha\).

Strategy : master each tool on the particle first, then extend it by adding the rotational term.

Velocity and acceleration are successive time derivatives of position

General definitions

\[\mathbf v = \frac{d\mathbf r}{dt},\qquad \mathbf a = \frac{d\mathbf v}{dt} = \frac{d^2\mathbf r}{dt^2}\]

Straight-line motion

\[v = \frac{dx}{dt},\qquad a = \frac{dv}{dt}\]
\[a = v\,\frac{dv}{dx}\]

Why \(a = v\,dv/dx\)? : when \(a\) is given as a function of position, this form eliminates time.

Constant acceleration, \(a = \text{const}\)

\[v = v_0 + a t\]
\[x = x_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac12 a t^2\]
\[v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\,(x - x_0)\]

Warning : these three equations hold only when \(a\) is constant. For \(a(t)\), \(a(v)\) or \(a(x)\), integrate the definitions directly.

Choosing the coordinate system is the key step in curvilinear problems

Cartesian (x–y)

\[\mathbf v = \dot x\,\mathbf i + \dot y\,\mathbf j,\qquad \mathbf a = \ddot x\,\mathbf i + \ddot y\,\mathbf j\]

Normal–tangential (n–t)

\[\mathbf a = a_t\,\mathbf e_t + a_n\,\mathbf e_n,\qquad a_t = \dot v\]
\[a_n = \frac{v^2}{\rho}\]

Circular motion (\(\rho = r\)) : \(\ a_n = \dfrac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r\)

Meaning of \(a_n\) : even at constant speed, a change of direction is an acceleration.

\[\sum F_t = m\dot v,\qquad \sum F_n = m\frac{v^2}{\rho}\]

Habit : draw the FBD before writing any equation — missing or duplicated forces are the most common source of error.

Relates position and speed directly — no time variable needed

\[T_1 + U_{1\to2} = T_2\]

Kinetic energy and work

\[T = \tfrac12 m v^2,\qquad U_{1\to2} = \int_1^2 \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r\]

Potential energy

\[V_g = m g h,\qquad V_e = \tfrac12 k x^2\]

Only conservative forces

\[T_1 + V_1 = T_2 + V_2\]

Scalar equation : no vector components and no reaction forces that do no work (normal force on a smooth path).

When to use : "find the speed, the distance is given, time is not needed" → try energy before Newton's law.

Short, large forces and collisions are handled with momentum

\[m\mathbf v_1 + \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\sum\mathbf F\,dt = m\mathbf v_2\]

Definitions

Linear momentum : \(\ \mathbf p = m\mathbf v\)

Impulse : \(\ \displaystyle\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\mathbf F\,dt\)

No external impulse

\[m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2 = m_1 v_1' + m_2 v_2'\]

Coefficient of restitution

\[e = \frac{v_2' - v_1'}{v_1 - v_2} = \frac{\text{separation}}{\text{approach}}\]

\(e = 1\) : perfectly elastic
\(e = 0\) : perfectly plastic

Meriam & Kraige, Fig. 3/17

Collision recipe : momentum conservation + restitution give two equations for \(v_1'\) and \(v_2'\).

Reading the table : every particle result has a rotational twin — replace \(m\) by \(I\), \(F\) by \(M\), \(a\) by \(\alpha\).

Velocity of B = velocity of A + rotation of B about A

\[\mathbf v_B = \mathbf v_A + \boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r_{B/A}\]

Two parts : translation with the reference point A, plus circular motion of B about A.

\(\mathbf v_{B/A} = \boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r_{B/A}\) : magnitude \(r\omega\), perpendicular to \(\mathbf r_{B/A}\).

Meriam & Kraige, Fig. 5/5 — (a) displacement, (b) motion relative to B, (c) motion relative to A

Why it matters : this is the most used equation in rigid-body kinematics — linkages, wheels and gears all reduce to it.

Relative acceleration adds a tangential term \(r\alpha\) and a normal term \(r\omega^2\)

Priority : general plane motion is the most important case — most exam problems (wheels, linkages, disks) belong here.

At each instant, a body in plane motion rotates about one point C where \(v_C = 0\)

\[v_A = \omega\, r_{A/C},\qquad v_B = \omega\, r_{B/C}\]
\[\omega = \frac{v_A}{r_{A/C}} = \frac{v_B}{r_{B/C}}\]
Meriam & Kraige, Fig. 5/7 — C lies on the normals to \(\mathbf v_A\) and \(\mathbf v_B\)

Locating C : draw lines perpendicular to \(\mathbf v_A\) and \(\mathbf v_B\); they intersect at C (a). Parallel velocities: use their magnitudes (b), (c).

Benefit : the velocity of any point follows from one distance — no vector algebra.

Caution : the IC is for velocity only. C itself generally accelerates, so \(a = \omega^2 r\) about C is wrong.

The contact point has zero velocity, which ties the motion of G to \(\omega\)

\[v_G = R\,\omega,\qquad a_G = R\,\alpha\]
Kochmann, ETH Dynamics lecture notes, p. 84 — rim point traces a cycloid; its speed is 0 at contact, \(2R\omega\) at the top

Common trap : zero velocity at C does not mean zero acceleration. C accelerates toward G with \(a_C = R\,\omega^2\).

Friction : static friction at C does no work, because C does not move at that instant.

Meriam & Kraige, Fig. 6/4 — forces on the FBD ≡ \(m\bar{\mathbf a}\) and \(\bar I\alpha\) on the kinetic diagram

Starting point : almost every rigid-body kinetics problem starts from these two equations.

Kinetic energy has two parts: translation of G and rotation about G

\[T = \tfrac12 m v_G^2 + \tfrac12 I_G\,\omega^2,\qquad T_1 + U_{1\to2} = T_2\]

Example: solid disk rolling from rest down a height \(h\)

\[T = \tfrac12 m v^2 + \tfrac12\left(\tfrac12 m R^2\right)\left(\tfrac{v}{R}\right)^2 = \tfrac34 m v^2\]
\[m g h = \tfrac34 m v^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{\tfrac43\, g h}\qquad \left(\text{sliding block: } v = \sqrt{2 g h}\right)\]

Rolling recipe : \(T = \tfrac12 m v_G^2 + \tfrac12 I_G\omega^2\) together with \(v_G = R\omega\) solves most rolling problems.

Interpretation : rotation stores part of the energy, so the rolling disk arrives slower than a frictionless sliding block.

Rigid bodies need both linear and angular momentum

Linear\(\displaystyle m\,\mathbf v_{G1} + \int\sum\mathbf F\,dt = m\,\mathbf v_{G2}\)
Angular (G)\(\displaystyle I_G\,\omega_1 + \int\sum M_G\,dt = I_G\,\omega_2\)

No external moment impulse

\[I_1\,\omega_1 = I_2\,\omega_2\]

A spinning skater pulls in the arms: \(I\) drops, so \(\omega\) rises.

Typical uses

Impacts on bars and pendulums, spinning bodies that change shape, coupled rotors.

Conservation : with no external moment impulse, angular momentum is conserved — even when energy is not.

Particle dynamics is \(F = ma\) seen through motion, energy and momentum

  • Kinematics describes motion; kinetics explains it through forces and moments
    • Choose coordinates to fit the path: Cartesian, n–t, or r–θ
  • Three kinetics methods for a particle
    • \(\sum F = ma\) · \(T_1 + U_{1\to2} = T_2\) · \(m v_1 + \int F\,dt = m v_2\)
  • Rigid-body dynamics adds rotation: \(\omega,\ \alpha,\ I\) and \(\sum M_G = I_G\alpha\)
    • Kinematics: \(\mathbf v_B = \mathbf v_A + \boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r_{B/A}\), IC, rolling \(v_G = R\omega\)
    • Energy and momentum each gain a rotational term: \(\tfrac12 I_G\omega^2\), \(I_G\,\omega\)
  • Decide the method first, then write the equation
