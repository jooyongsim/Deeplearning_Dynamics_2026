Prerequisites : \(\sum\mathbf F=m\mathbf a\) and impulse–momentum from the dynamics lecture, vectors and matrices, basic Python.

A computer cannot integrate continuously — it advances the state in small steps \(\Delta t\)

Continuous

\[m\frac{d\mathbf v}{dt}=\sum\mathbf F,\qquad \frac{d\mathbf x}{dt}=\mathbf v\]

Discrete: semi-implicit Euler

\[\mathbf v_{k+1}=\mathbf v_k+\frac{\sum\mathbf F}{m}\,\Delta t\]
\[\mathbf x_{k+1}=\mathbf x_k+\mathbf v_{k+1}\,\Delta t\]

Semi-implicit : the position uses the new velocity — stable for springs and PD controllers where explicit Euler gains energy.

Two friction models : the floor uses this simple viscous law; the pusher–T contact uses Coulomb friction \(\lvert F_t\rvert\le\mu F_n\) (later).

Three numbers place the T, \((x_G,y_G,\theta)\); three more describe its motion, \((\mathbf v_G,\omega)\)

Equations of motion (plane)

\[\sum\mathbf F=m\,\mathbf a_G,\qquad \sum M_G=I_G\,\alpha\]

Where is a body point?

\[\mathbf p=\mathbf x_G+R(\theta)\,\mathbf p_{\text{local}},\quad R(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}\]

Rotation is integrated like translation

\[\omega_{k+1}=\omega_k+\alpha\Delta t,\qquad \theta_{k+1}=\theta_k+\omega_{k+1}\Delta t\]

In PushT : apart from floor damping, every push reaches the T as an impulse — so \(\mathbf v_G\) and \(\omega\) change by jumps (Steps 5–6).

Every point shares \(\mathbf v_G\), plus a rotational part perpendicular to \(\mathbf r\)

\[\mathbf v_C=\mathbf v_G+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r,\qquad \mathbf r=\mathbf x_C-\mathbf x_G\]

In 2D, \(\boldsymbol\omega=(0,0,\omega)\)

\[\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r=(-\omega r_y,\ \omega r_x),\qquad \mathbf r\times\mathbf F=r_xF_y-r_yF_x\]

Worked example

\(\mathbf v_G=(1,0)\) m/s, \(\omega=2\) rad/s, \(\mathbf r=(0,\,0.5)\) m

\[\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r=(-1,\,0)\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbf v_C=(0,\,0)\]

That point is momentarily at rest — the instantaneous centre of the dynamics lecture.

def omega_cross_r(omega, r):

"""[0, 0, omega] x [rx, ry, 0]"""

return np.array([-omega * r[1], omega * r[0]])

def cross2(a, b):

"""2D cross product a x b -> scalar (z)"""

return float(a[0] * b[1] - a[1] * b[0])

# used for the contact point:

v_C = body.vel + omega_cross_r(body.omega, r)

Why it matters : a collision happens at the contact point, so its speed — not the COM speed — decides whether the bodies approach.

A collision force is huge and brief — integrate it once and change the velocity in a jump

\[\mathbf J=\int_{t}^{t+\epsilon}\mathbf F\,dt\]

Linear and angular impulse–momentum

\[m\mathbf v_G^{+}=m\mathbf v_G^{-}+\mathbf J\]
\[I_G\,\omega^{+}=I_G\,\omega^{-}+\mathbf r\times\mathbf J\]

Worked example (T: \(m=1,\ I_G=0.434\))

\(\mathbf J=(0.5,\,0)\) N·s at \(\mathbf r=(0,\,-0.5)\) m : \(\ \Delta\mathbf v_G=(0.5,\,0)\) m/s, \(\ \mathbf r\times\mathbf J=0.25\ \Rightarrow\ \Delta\omega=0.58\) rad/s

Closed loop : the policy sees the result of its last action, so it can correct its own mistakes.

Deterministic vs. stochastic : \(a=\pi(o)\) or \(a\sim\pi(a\mid o)\) — the second is needed for multimodal demos and for RL exploration.

Fit a function to examples \((x,y)\) by following the gradient of a loss

Loss on a minibatch of \(B\) examples

\[\mathcal L(\phi)=\frac1B\sum_{i=1}^{B}\big\lVert f_\phi(x_i)-y_i\big\rVert^2\]

Gradient descent

\[\phi\leftarrow\phi-\eta\,\nabla_\phi\mathcal L(\phi)\]

Minibatch \(B=256\), drawn at random every step. AdamW adapts the step per parameter. Validation set : episodes never trained on.

pusht_imitation.py : MLP · BCPolicy

class MLP(nn.Module):

def __init__(self, d_in, d_out, width=256, depth=3):

layers, d = [], d_in

for _ in range(depth):

layers += [nn.Linear(d, width), nn.Mish()]

d = width

self.net = nn.Sequential(*layers, nn.Linear(d, d_out))

class BCPolicy(Normalized):

def loss(self, x, y): # MSE, normalized

return ((self.f(self.nx(x)) - self.ny(y)) ** 2).mean()

Why a nonlinearity : without \(\sigma\) the whole stack collapses into one linear map — it could not represent "push left of the T here, right of it there".

Behavior cloning = supervised learning with \(x=\) observation and \(y=\) expert action

\[\min_\phi\ \mathbb E_{(o,a^{*})\sim\mathcal D_{\text{expert}}}\big\lVert\pi_\phi(o)-a^{*}\big\rVert^2\]

Covariate shift

Training states come from the expert; at test time states come from the policy itself. One small error leads to a state the demos never showed, where the next error is larger.

\[\text{mistakes over }T\text{ steps: BC}\sim T^2\epsilon,\quad \text{DAgger}\sim T\epsilon\]

Ross & Bagnell (2010); Ross, Gordon & Bagnell (2011)

Two more problems

Multimodality : the MSE optimum is the mean of the demonstrated actions (next slides).

No recovery data : we save only successful demos, so the policy never sees how to fix a bad push.

Remedies in this course : action chunks, Diffusion Policy for multimodality — and RL fine-tuning, which learns from the policy's own states.

Markov property : the next state depends only on \((s,a)\) — that is why velocities belong in the state, or two frames in the observation.

The agent maximizes the discounted sum of rewards, not the next reward

\[G_t=r_t+\gamma r_{t+1}+\gamma^2r_{t+2}+\dots=\sum_{k\ge0}\gamma^k r_{t+k}\]
\[G_t=r_t+\gamma\,G_{t+1}\qquad(\text{recursive})\]
\[J(\phi)=\mathbb E_{\tau\sim\pi_\phi}\big[G_0\big]\ \to\ \max_\phi\]
\(\gamma=0.99\)Value
effective horizon \(1/(1-\gamma)\)100 steps = 10 s
bonus 10 after 100 steps\(10\cdot0.99^{100}=3.7\)
bonus 10 after 50 steps\(10\cdot0.99^{50}=6.1\)

Discounting : a success that comes sooner is worth more — together with the −0.01 time penalty, this is why RL learns to finish faster than the expert.

\(\tau\) : one trajectory \((s_0,a_0,r_0,s_1,\dots)\) produced by running the policy.

How good is a state? The return the policy can expect from there

\[V^{\pi}(s)=\mathbb E_\pi\big[G_t\mid s_t=s\big]\]

Bellman equation (from \(G_t=r_t+\gamma G_{t+1}\))

\[V^{\pi}(s)=\mathbb E\big[r_t+\gamma V^{\pi}(s_{t+1})\mid s_t=s\big]\]

Temporal-difference (TD) error

\[\delta_t=r_t+\gamma V(s_{t+1})-V(s_t)\]

\(\delta_t>0\) : the step went better than the critic expected.

Was this action better or worse than what the policy usually does in this state?

\[A^{\pi}(s,a)=Q^{\pi}(s,a)-V^{\pi}(s),\qquad \hat A_t^{(1)}=\delta_t\]

\[\hat A_t=\sum_{l\ge0}(\gamma\lambda)^l\,\delta_{t+l}=\delta_t+\gamma\lambda\,\hat A_{t+1}\]

Make better-than-usual actions more likely, and worse-than-usual ones less likely

Gaussian policy for continuous actions

\[\pi_\phi(a\mid s)=\mathcal N\big(\mu_\phi(s),\sigma^2\big)\]
\[\log\pi_\phi(a\mid s)=-\frac{\lVert a-\mu_\phi(s)\rVert^2}{2\sigma^2}+\text{const}\]

Policy-gradient theorem

\[\nabla_\phi J=\mathbb E\big[\nabla_\phi\log\pi_\phi(a_t\mid s_t)\,\hat A_t\big]\]

\(\hat A>0\) : move \(\mu_\phi(s)\) toward the tried action. \(\hat A<0\) : move it away.

Compare with BC : BC pulls \(\mu\) toward the expert's action; policy gradient pulls it toward the agent's own actions, weighted by how well they worked.

RL can only improve what it has tried — success has to happen at least sometimes

Sparse vs. shaped reward

Sparse : +10 only at success. A random pusher practically never aligns the T within 0.15 m and 10° → no learning signal.

Shaped : \(10\,(c(s_t)-c(s_{t+1}))\) every step. Potential-based, so the best policy is unchanged (Ng et al., 1999).
